A data story · Pairwise70 family

Begin with a number that should be ordinary: 0.005.

It is the gap below which two meta-analyses agree. The question this whole family of work circles back to is small enough to fit in one line — and stubborn enough to need seven projects to answer.

Can a reader reproduce what a meta-analysis claims?

First, the ground.

Not one trial, not one review — a corpus. It arrives in steps, each larger than the last.

Picture a surveyor.

She walks a coastline and measures it to the nearest mile. Back at her desk she draws a beautiful map and marks it to the nearest foot. The map is precise. The coastline was never measured that finely. The precision lives on the page, not in the world.

Re-pool every Cochrane analysis from the numbers a reader can actually lift off the page, and a floor appears: one analysis in seven cannot be landed back on its own published value.

Where the data were counts, the floor held. Where they were means, it doubled.

The same question, asked of different data.

Binary outcomes — events counted — reproduce best. Continuous and generic-inverse-variance outcomes, built from means and standard errors, carry less recoverable precision, and the floor rises.

Now it is your turn.

You have the forest plot. You extract the per-trial numbers. You re-pool them yourself. One time in seven, you will not arrive where the paper stands — not because you erred, but because the precision was never carried in the figures you were given.

Browse the corpus yourself.

Pick any of these reviews — and the entire deep-dive redraws from its real data: the forest of outcomes, and for its largest outcome the funnel, prediction interval, leave-one-out, cumulative, GOSH, and Bayesian posterior. Hover any point for its study and numbers.

And it generalises.

The same machinery reads any review. Here is a different one entirely — unopposed estrogen and endometrial hyperplasia — split by dose. Every stratum shows sharply raised risk, a dose-response pattern a single pooled number would have flattened.

So the family was built.

Seven instruments around one bench: the corpus that holds the evidence, the atlas that measured the floor, the engines that pool and stress-test, the robust estimators that argue with the mean.

One analysis, in full.

This is not a sketch. Here are six outcomes from a single real Cochrane review in the corpus — pooled as risk ratios, each estimate validated against metafor to the sixth decimal. Left of the line favours treatment.

And inside one outcome.

Zoom into the first row — all-cause mortality. Behind that single pooled diamond sit thirteen real trials, each a point in the funnel: the precise ones near the top, the small ones fanning out below. This is the raw material the reproduction floor is measured against.

A second opinion.

Ask the same question in Bayesian terms — a random-effects model with a weakly informative prior on between-study spread — and the posterior tells the same story: a credible interval for the pooled risk ratio that, like the prediction interval, includes 1.

Then stress-test it.

Does any single trial hold up the result? Leave each one out in turn — the pooled risk ratio barely moves. And across every two-or-more-trial combination of the thirteen, the estimate stays left of 1: a finding that does not depend on which studies you happen to trust.

Return to the number.

0.005 is not a flaw to be patched; it is a limit to be known. The point was never to win the argument — only to make it reproducible.

Can a reader reproduce it? Now — in one program — you can try.

Narrative method

This page is built with classical narrative devices, used purely as craft: ring composition (it opens and closes on the same number), a recurring refrain repeated with variation, a parable to carry an abstract idea, gradual disclosure of the figures, a shift of address from "they" to "you", and contrast pairs (counts vs. means). No content here is religious; the techniques are rhetorical only.

What can go wrong in a Cochrane meta-analysis

A working taxonomy of the ways a pooled estimate can mislead. The first three panels are computed from this corpus; the rest are well-established methodological concerns, each linked to the family project that probes it.

Publication bias, adjusted — a worked example

A real outcome that does show funnel asymmetry. Trim-and-fill imputes the "missing" small negative trials and re-pools — a sensitivity check on how much small-study bias may inflate the result.

GRADE — certainty of the evidence (automated screen)

A GRADE-style read of the domains that can be computed from the data (inconsistency, imprecision, publication bias). Risk-of-bias and indirectness need manual review and are marked "not assessed" — so these are provisional, not a published GRADE rating.
